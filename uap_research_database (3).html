▸ Galileo Project (Harvard)
est. 2021 Public Active
Focus
University-hosted search for extraterrestrial technosignatures near Earth; now also seeding the new UAP Science Advisory Council advising U.S. agencies. Operates multiple ground-based, multimodal observatories with published sensor specifications. Cross-references: (1) its SkyWatch passive radar (below) was developed and proof-of-concept-deployed by Mitch Randall as a Galileo Project Research Team member starting 2021 — the same Randall who separately proposes 'Operation Skywatch,' a citizen-deployable consumer version, elsewhere in this database; (2) Gerry Tedesco (Nightcrawler entry, below) is listed in his 2026 UAP Summit speaker bio as a Galileo Project 'Scientific Researcher' under Dr. Avi Loeb — a second direct personnel link between Galileo and an independent citizen group in this database.
Methods / equipment
Wide-field optical + IR arrays, radio antennas, audio sensors, custom vector magnetometers. Publishes openly and welcomes public data contribution, but is centrally directed by Harvard-affiliated staff rather than an open-source hardware/software project or a distributed volunteer network — it's public-facing, not structurally decentralized. Major update from a verbatim transcript of a 2025 Sol Foundation talk by Dr. Avi Loeb himself (via a working YouTube-transcript tool). Real operational output confirmed: the observatory collects data on roughly 100,000 objects per month; two additional observatories were under construction as of this talk, targeting millions of monitored objects by summer 2025. Real commissioning-paper findings, led by named researcher Laura Domini (who has assembled a team of undergraduates/researchers): of 500,000 objects analyzed in the first infrared-only commissioning dataset, 16% showed non-straight-line motion, and about 144 remained genuinely ambiguous (unexplained) due to lack of a distance estimate — explicitly framed by Loeb as early/limited results, not a claimed detection. Real calibration methodology: instrument efficiency and detection acceptance were measured using known aircraft positions from ADS-B/radio transponder data as ground truth. Planned near-term upgrade (as of this talk, 'in the coming weeks'): deploying multiple camera units separated by a few miles to triangulate distance via stereo geometry (explicitly analogized to binocular vision), plus a second, independent distance-estimation method under development by Domini's team using the propagation-speed difference between light and sound (sound travels roughly a million times slower than light — the same principle behind seeing lightning before hearing thunder) by fusing acoustic sensor data with optical/infrared detections. Separately, Loeb detailed the Galileo Project's 2023 Pacific Ocean expedition to recover material from IM1, an interstellar meteor (detected roughly a decade before this talk, catalogued via NASA's CNEOS database, moving faster than 95% of nearby stars, exploding ~20km above the Pacific releasing roughly 1% of the Hiroshima bomb's energy, with its interstellar origin confirmed in a letter from US Space Command to NASA). The June 2023 expedition cost $1.5 million, funded by a donation from Charles Hoskinson (co-founder of Ethereum and Cardano), aboard a ship named Silver Star, using a magnet-covered sled dragged across roughly 7 square miles of ocean floor at about 1 mile depth to collect material. Findings: 850 total 'spherules' (molten droplets under 1mm) were recovered — most found by named summer intern Sophie Bergstrom, who found roughly 10x more than were found aboard the ship itself. About 10% showed an anomalous elemental composition (named 'BeLaU' for beryllium/lanthanum/uranium enrichment) with certain heavy elements up to 1,000x more abundant than typical solar-system material, analyzed by named Harvard geochemist Stein Jacobsen. Loeb directly addressed two public counter-theories: that the material was ordinary coal ash (he states compositional analysis showed a clear difference from coal ash), and that the detected signal source was a truck rather than the meteor explosion (he notes government satellite sensors, not the disputed ground sensor, were what confirmed the explosion). A follow-up expedition was planned for summer 2025, budgeted at $6.5 million and explicitly unfunded as of this talk, intended to deploy a remotely-operated vehicle with video feed to attempt recovery of larger wreckage pieces. Also referenced: the Vera Rubin Observatory (NSF-funded, Chile, not a Galileo Project instrument but a related future data source) — confirmed specs: a 3.2-gigapixel camera, monitoring the southern sky roughly every 4 days, expected to detect new interstellar objects (like 'Oumuamua) at a rate of roughly one every few months once operational.
  • All-sky IR camera array — 8× uncooled long-wave infrared FLIR Boson 640 cameras, extrinsically calibrated using synchronized ADS-B aircraft-position data. Matches an instrument Loeb described in a 2025 talk (nickname unclear in the transcript, described only as resembling 'R2D2 from Star Wars') with 8 cameras monitoring the full sky continuously, distinct from standard astronomical observatories that only cover a small sky fraction — this appears to be the same array, now with confirmed real operational throughput (~100,000 objects/month) and commissioning-paper results (500,000 objects analyzed, 16% non-straight-line motion, ~144 unexplained).
  • Wide-field optical camera array — Continuous full-sky optical imaging paired with the IR array at the Harvard observatory site.
  • Radio-band sensors — Monitors nearby-sky radio emissions as part of the multi-band observatory suite.
  • Broadband acoustic system — Continuous audio monitoring, cross-referenced against optical/IR detections. Per a 2025 Loeb talk, being developed into a multimodal (acoustic + infrared/optical) machine learning distance-estimation system by researcher Laura Domini's team, using the light-vs-sound propagation-speed difference.
  • Weather-monitoring station — Logs temperature, pressure, humidity, and wind for environmental context and false-positive screening.
  • Vector magnetometer / geomagnetic variometer station — Deployed at the Colorado observatory to flag magnetic anomalies uncorrelated with known geomagnetic storm activity.
  • SkyWatch passive multistatic radar — Network of receivers using commercial FM radio transmitters of opportunity; tracks 3D position/velocity of objects out to ~150 km range and 80 km altitude.
  • ADS-B receivers — Used for calibration and to deconflict known aircraft from candidate detections.
  • ML classification pipeline — Custom computer vision/ML system that filters birds, insects, balloons, drones, and aircraft out of the continuous data stream.
  • Magnetic collection sled (Pacific Expedition, 2023) — A magnet-covered sled dragged across the Pacific Ocean floor (~1 mile depth) to collect material from the IM1 interstellar meteor debris field; recovered 850 'spherules' across a ~7 square mile survey area during a $1.5M June 2023 expedition.

▸ UAPx, Inc. (original nonprofit)
est. 2021 Volunteer Active
Focus
Florida-based 501(c)(3) all-volunteer scientific research organization, formed in 2021 by Navy veterans Kevin Day, Gary Voorhis, and Jason Turner — all three served aboard the USS Princeton during the 2004 'Nimitz/Tic Tac' encounters — to fill the civilian sensor-data gap identified by the 2021 UAP Task Force report.
Methods / equipment
Field expeditions with visible/near-IR PTZ cameras (UFODAP/OSIRIS hardware), UV/IR imaging, radiological measurement devices, non-EM emission sensors. Ran its flagship July 2021 Catalina Channel expedition in partnership with UAlbany SUNY physicists — that partnership has since been formalized into the separate 'UAlbany Project X' entity below. Substantial update from a verbatim transcript of a full documentary covering the 2021 Catalina expedition ('A Tear In The Sky,' via a working YouTube-transcript tool). Confirms Matthew Szydagis by name and role as a real UAPx team member — associate professor of physics, expertise in dark matter/radiation detection — resolving a name previously logged as uncertain from a separate Kevin Knuth talk. Also names additional team members: Dave Altman and Michael Hall (experienced UFO researchers), and David Mason (inventor of the team's light/sound communication devices, detailed below). Team split across two-plus locations for triangulation: a mainland site (Laguna area) and a rooftop position on Catalina Island, with a third mobile 'truck' position, specifically to enable cross-angle correlation of any detected event. Specific dated events from the expedition, July 14, 2021: at 5:26pm, a 'pink object' observed making a sharp turn and exiting frame; at 6:07pm, a high-energy radiation event recorded on the Cosmic Watch at 43.37 MeV (stated as the highest of the entire week-long expedition, against a normal terrestrial background of roughly 2 MeV); at 6:09pm, an elongated object captured on FLIR moving laterally against the prevailing wind direction — a genuine aerodynamic anomaly if the object were unpowered. Separately and independently, at 9:29pm, the Catalina Island team (a different location and angle from the mainland FLIR site) captured a solid, cold-registering object on two night-vision cameras and one CCD camera simultaneously — timing-correlated with a separate anomalous elongated object recorded on FLIR at the mainland site, interpreted by the team as likely the same object viewed from two triangulated positions across three different sensor types (visible, and two kinds of infrared). A specific, methodologically serious event: a 'hole in the clouds' captured by a UFODAP camera at approximately 3:59-4:01am, closing within a fraction of a second. Using basic trigonometry (known camera field of view, elevation angle, and a measured cloud ceiling of ~1,700 feet), the team calculated the hole was roughly 1.2km offshore, elliptical (~0.4km by 0.1km), with a closure speed implying roughly 700mph — explicitly ruled out as a natural 'fallstreak hole' (cloud ceiling too low/warm for the ice-crystal mechanism that produces those) or an aircraft-punched hole (no aircraft present per ADS-B/radar at the time). The event coincided with the same peak 43.37 MeV Cosmic Watch radiation spike. All eight FLIR cameras (fed from two independent power sources) lost power roughly seven minutes before the event — explicitly flagged by the team as suspicious rather than dismissed as routine equipment failure, though presented as an observation, not a proven causal claim. Cross-checked against outside data sources: weather radar showed an actual reflective-object cluster at the same location; NASA's Fermi gamma-ray space telescope showed no correlation with any known distant astrophysical gamma-ray burst; no correlation found with solar activity. The team explicitly hedged their own 'wormhole' framing, calling it 'a new phenomenon' rather than confirmed evidence of non-human intelligence, and noting camera malfunction was ruled out via control comparisons (identical equipment functioning normally minutes earlier, correctly triggering on birds/planes elsewhere in the same time window, and a separate identical patch of sky showing no anomaly at the same time). A second specific claimed observation: objects appearing to fall from the sky into the ocean, calculated via camera field-of-view/horizon-distance trigonometry to be at least 4 feet across falling at a minimum of 5,000mph (nearer objects) and up to 69 feet across falling at a minimum of 84,000mph (more distant objects) — the team explicitly noted that even a 50% margin of calculation error would still leave extraordinary values. Stated cross-validation: these size/speed estimates were reported as falling within the same range as separate, independent findings from the Scientific Coalition for UAP Studies (SCU, elsewhere in this database) and from Kevin Knuth's own published kinematic analysis — a real, if self-reported, cross-check between two different groups already in this database. Historical context cited by the team: a 1951 radar/visual case with a pilot-estimated speed of ~1,500mph, radar-tracked (Newfoundland) at ~1,800mph — notably two years before the human airspeed record (695mph) was broken; and declassified FOIA-released reports from the late 1940s/early 1950s describing 'flying white butane tanks,' a shape description resembling modern 'Tic Tac' reports.
  • UFODAP / OSIRIS multi-sensor camera units — PTZ visible + near-IR cameras with OTDAU (Optical Tracking Data Acquisition Unit) machine-vision software for automated target tracking; hardware built by Ron Olch. Confirmed via documentary transcript: includes integrated magnetometers, GPS, wireless connectivity, barometric pressure/weather sensors, and ADS-B tracking for known-aircraft correlation. Described as 'the little brother' of a larger companion system called 'the Osiris,' which can remotely monitor/control the smaller units.
  • FLIR thermal cameras — Gimbal-mounted infrared cameras, upgraded post-2021 to lightweight smartphone-slaved models that can co-track with the UFODAP unit. Confirmed via documentary transcript: an array of 8 FLIR cameras was run simultaneously for full-sky coverage during the 2021 expedition. Multiple unexplained objects were recorded registering unusually cold — as low as -20°F, with at least one event down to -80°F — described by the team as showing no exhaust signature and no explanation for the extreme cold reading.
  • UV imaging camera — Added after the first expedition to probe UV as a largely unexplored band for UAP signatures.
  • MIT Cosmic Watch — Cosmic-ray/particle detector used to log background radiation and flag energetic-particle anomalies; recorded ~55 hours of background radiation data on the first expedition. Confirmed via documentary transcript: outputs are monitored both visually (event log) and audibly, with gamma radiation events described as 'chirps' and beta radiation as 'clicks.' Peak recorded event during the 2021 expedition: 43.37 MeV, against a normal terrestrial background of roughly 2 MeV.
  • Custom quantum random number generator (QRNG) — Built by UAPx quantum technologist Christopher Altman as a statistical baseline instrument to compare against anomalous environmental readings.
  • Radiological measurement devices — COTS and internally-developed detectors 'inspired by MIT, FLIR, NASA Ames' per UAPx's own description. Documentary transcript confirms a separate general-purpose radiation detector, distinct from the Cosmic Watch, described as designed by 'friends and colleagues at MIT' specifically to flag any reading anomalous above normal background at a given location.
  • Infrasound and seismograph sensors — Used to cross-check candidate events against low-frequency acoustic and ground-motion data.
  • External data cross-referencing — Correlates on-site sensor data with radar, satellite imagery, Doppler weather radar, and compiled SIGINT/MASINT feeds. Documentary transcript confirms specific real-world use: NASA's Fermi gamma-ray telescope data and solar activity records were checked against a candidate radiation event, and weather radar was checked against a candidate visual anomaly, as part of ruling out mundane explanations.
  • LightWave transmitter (David Mason) — A device, connected to a laser, designed to detect potential light-pulsation communication patterns from a UAP, convert them to audible sound for human pattern-recognition, and rebroadcast the received pattern back toward the source as an attempted 'hello world' handshake protocol. Covers a broad range including radio/microwave, optical/UV/visible/infrared light, and temperature. Confirmed as continuously operational (24/7) during the 2021 expedition.
  • Handheld lithium-powered nuclear fusion reactor (in development, patent pending) — Per a transcript-verified 2025 Sol Foundation talk by Kevin Knuth (physicist, confirmed UAPx team member). Developed by Matthew Szydagis — now independently confirmed by name and role (associate professor of physics, dark matter/radiation detection expertise) via the 2021 expedition documentary, resolving an earlier uncertain name. Stated rationale: UAP have been repeatedly observed showing apparent interest in or ability to detect nuclear materials (weapons, storage depots) — mechanism unknown (neutrinos and gamma rays both suggested as possibilities, neither confirmed) — so the device is intended to power UAPx's field observation equipment specifically as a deliberate 'lure.' Explicitly still in development and patent-pending as of the Knuth talk — not yet a confirmed, independently-verified, operational device.
Not to be confused with UAlbany Project X (UAPx) or UAPx.io below — same brand name, three distinct entities. See notes on each. Per the same Knuth talk: a new paper covering initial results from UAPx's first field expeditions was stated as forthcoming 'early next year' as of that talk. Also worth noting, per the documentary transcript: a specific cross-camera discrepancy was recorded — a candidate object appeared on a PTZ camera but did not appear on a fisheye (all-sky) camera positioned within two feet of it, pointed at the same patch of sky — logged by the team as an unresolved anomaly in its own right, not just a candidate UAP sighting.

▸ Project Hessdalen (Norway)
est. 1983 Volunteer Active
Focus
One of the longest continuously running, most heavily instrumented independent UAP field-research programs in the world — studying the 'Hessdalen Lights' in a Norwegian valley since 1983. Became a formal non-profit citizen-science organization in 2023. Not affiliated with the Lund University project above (different country, founders, and methodology) despite occupying similar 'Scandinavian UAP research' territory. Formal partnership with the Scientific Coalition for UAP Studies (SCU) since 2023, plus a long-running data-sharing collaboration with France's GEIPAN.
Methods / equipment
Continuous automated field monitoring via 'the Blue Box' — described by the project as the world's first fully autonomous multi-sensor anomaly detection system, running since 1998 — supplemented by open field trips, an annual science camp for students (since 2002), workshops, and conferences. Meetings are open to the public and volunteers can join the field team directly.
  • The Blue Box — Fully autonomous, multi-sensor anomaly detection station monitoring the Hessdalen sky continuously since 1998 — the project's core, always-on instrument.
  • Multi-instrument 1984 field survey suite — Original field investigation used cameras, radar, magnetometers, infrared viewers, seismographs, lasers, and Geiger counters — establishing the multi-modal approach the project still follows.

▸ Magnetic Gradiometry for Transient Phenomena: Model-Fitting Breaks Down, and 'Baseline' Isn't a Quiet Canvas
Magnetic / Geophysical Sensing Partially mitigated
The issue
Two compounding problems with using magnetometer/gradiometer arrays (the 'Magnetic Anomaly Detection' or MAD approach, long used for submarine detection) to catch transient UAP-related magnetic disturbances. First: MAD's proven detection algorithms (Orthogonal Basis Function decomposition, Levenberg-Marquardt localization) work by fitting sensor data to a known, persistent target model — a submarine is a large, continuous ferromagnetic dipole that holds still long enough to integrate signal over many seconds and fit to an assumed shape. A transient, unmodeled UAP-hypothesized signature has no extended dwell time to integrate over and no known target shape to fit against, so the model-fitting techniques that make MAD work in practice don't transfer. Second: the standard background-subtraction approach (comparing live readings against a reference model like the IGRF) doesn't produce a 'quiet canvas' to look for anomalies against — the IGRF specifically only models slow, multi-year secular variation in Earth's core-generated field, and was never designed to capture solar-flare or geomagnetic-storm-driven variability on minutes-to-days timescales (real-time space weather uses separate, faster-cadence products like Kp/Dst indices for that). Even a better real-time baseline is still a description of expected variability, not evidence of a quiet background — and if solar/geomagnetic activity is hypothesized to statistically co-occur with UAP sightings, subtracting that activity out as pure noise would systematically blind a detection system to exactly the correlation someone might be trying to study, rather than controlling for it as a covariate.
Why it matters
Undermines the core assumption behind proposing a passive, always-on, broad-area magnetometer/gradiometer network (whether citizen-operated or otherwise) as a reliable way to detect and localize transient UAP-related magnetic events. The technique this is modeled on (MAD) has never actually been proven to work in that deployment mode — see 'established precedent' below.
Established precedent / what's actually proven
Real-world MAD has only ever been proven in cued, close-range deployments against a known-shape, persistent target: aircraft/helicopter-mounted magnetometers flying at low altitude (~150m or less) directly over a location already suspected of holding a submarine (typically cued first by sonar or other intelligence), with detection ranges of only a few hundred feet even under optimal conditions. One patent notes Earth's ambient field intensity can be roughly 10,000 times stronger than a submarine's signature — MAD only works because of the sophisticated model-fitting math layered on top, not raw sensitivity. A static/distributed precedent does exist (harbor-defense buoy networks ringing a specific protected area, feeding data fusion), but even that patent's own description immediately flags 'several shortcomings' to the approach. No precedent exists for passive, broad-area, uncued detection of an unmodeled transient target.

▸ Thermal/FLIR Footage Is a Temperature Map, Not a Photograph — and It's Routinely Treated as One
Optical / Thermal Imaging Open
The issue
Publicly released and publicly discussed UAP thermal/infrared footage is very frequently interpreted, described, and debated as though it were a conventional photograph — i.e. as if the visible shape, edges, and 'features' in the frame directly represent the object's physical form. A thermal/FLIR sensor actually encodes relative temperature (or radiant flux) at each pixel, not reflected visible light. Object 'shape' in thermal imagery is really a heat-distribution map, which can be smaller, larger, blurrier, or differently-shaped than the object's actual physical silhouette depending on emissivity, atmospheric path effects, sensor resolution, and thermal blooming (a hot or cold point source can appear to 'bleed' into a larger blob than the true object size). Treating apparent thermal 'edges' as if they were the object's true physical boundary — the same way a viewer would read a visible-light photo — routinely leads to false claims about an object's shape, size, or lack of visible means of propulsion.
Why it matters
This affects the interpretation of essentially every thermal/FLIR-based UAP case in wide public circulation, including official government releases. It means claims like 'the object had no visible wings or engine' based on thermal footage are claims about the object's heat signature, not its physical structure — a materially different and much weaker claim than it's usually presented as. Multiple groups in this database field FLIR/thermal cameras as core equipment (Nightcrawler, Skinwalker Ranch, the Galileo Project, and others), making this a widely-applicable methodological caution rather than a criticism of any one group's hardware choice.
Established precedent / what's actually proven
Directly and explicitly stated by Dr. Jacques Vallée in a 2025 Sol Foundation interview (transcript-verified via a working YouTube-transcript tool): discussing AARO's May 9, 2025 public release of an 8-minute infrared UAP video (recorded by a US military drone over the Middle East in 2023, appearing to show an object moving at high speed with no discernible wings or propulsion), Vallée states plainly: 'those fuzzy black and white pictures that are not pictures... they are recordings of heat signatures. And everybody is treating it as if it was a picture.' AARO's own stated conclusion on that same footage was that 'the available data does not support a conclusive analytic evaluation' — i.e. even the agency that released it declined to draw firm conclusions from the thermal data alone.

▸ The Energy Deposition Problem: Where Does the Kinetic Energy Go When a UAP Decelerates?
Kinematics / Energetics Open
The issue
A specific, well-posed physics puzzle, distinct from (and arguably more rigorous than) the more commonly-discussed 'how did it accelerate that fast' question: when a claimed UAP undergoes extreme deceleration from a very high velocity to a stop, conservation of energy requires that kinetic energy to go somewhere — converted to heat, sound, light, or some other detectable form. In documented cases, no corresponding energy release (explosion, sonic boom, heat signature, fireball) has been observed to match the calculated energy involved.
Why it matters
This is a genuinely harder problem to wave away than raw acceleration claims, because it doesn't just ask 'what force could produce this motion' — it asks 'this amount of energy had to go somewhere, and it apparently didn't,' which is a stronger and more specific constraint. A propulsion system exotic enough to produce the acceleration still has to obey energy conservation on the way back down.
Established precedent / what's actually proven
Quantified directly in a transcript-verified 2025 Sol Foundation talk by physicist Kevin Knuth (via a working YouTube-transcript tool), analyzing the 2004 Nimitz 'Tic Tac' case: the object reportedly dropped from ~28,000 feet to sea level in 78 seconds. A simple kinematic estimate (accelerate for half the descent, decelerate for the other half) gives a minimum acceleration around 5,000 G — for comparison, an F-35's wings are rated to fail around 13 G. Assuming a mass of roughly 1,000 kg (one-tenth of an F/A-18's ~10,000 kg, used as a deliberately conservative lower bound), the implied power output comes out to roughly 1,100 gigawatts — more than 10x the total nuclear power output of the entire United States, from a single object the size of a fighter jet. Knuth's own novel point: that same energy had to be dissipated somewhere when the object decelerated to a stop — his estimate puts the equivalent energy release at roughly 250 simultaneous Tomahawk cruise missile detonations. No such explosion, fireball, or comparable energy signature was reported. Real historical corroborating data cited: Hermann Oberth (a father of modern rocketry, mentor to Wernher von Braun) referenced over 50 radar measurements as of a 1954 lecture showing similar objects traveling at ~19 km/s (~42,000 mph); a 1968 Minot AFB radar case (analyzed by French researcher Claude Poher) showed accelerations around 2,000-900 G with a top speed near 9,000 mph; the 1986 Japan Airlines Flight 1628 case (a real, extensively documented aviation radar-visual case), re-analyzed from the original radar dataset by a named Niels Bohr Institute researcher, showed 3 of 11 tracked maneuvers with accelerations of 9,000+ G and a top speed near 269,000 mph — with no sonic booms reported despite sustained hypersonic speed in atmosphere.

Directly relevant to UAPx, Inc. (Groups Database, elsewhere in this database) — Knuth is a named UAPx-affiliated researcher, and this analysis is part of the same broader research program. Also relevant to any group in this database citing extreme kinematic claims (Skywatcher's velocity-from-slant-range estimates, Nightcrawler's radar-tracked objects) as a standard this kind of claim should be checked against — an acceleration claim alone is a weaker, less complete claim than one paired with a corresponding accounting of where the kinetic energy went.
